Slide 5 · Strategy

Riverty’s First Consumer Card: the unexplained decline at the till

Problem hook

Riverty is losing the people who use it most, because the moment Lena wants to decide-then-pay in a shop or at a non-partner merchant, Riverty isn’t there and Klarna or PayPal gets the sale and the relationship, which, left alone, erodes the consumer pull that makes Riverty worth a merchant’s checkout.

Value proposition

When an in-store payment with the Riverty Card is declined, the app sends a lock-screen notice within seconds. It gives one plain-language reason and one next step: try again, enter your PIN, check your limit in the app, or pay another way. If the reason can’t be shared, the notice still says the payment was declined and what to try next, so no decline is ever silent. The change is to what the app shows. Card approval, limits, pricing and terms stay as they are.

FOR THE CUSTOMER
She can’t tell whether the card, the shop or the terminal failed, so she reaches for her debit card. With a reason and a step she can fix it on the spot and keep paying with Riverty.
FOR RIVERTY
Turning a failed payment into a successful Riverty purchase keeps the transaction and the relationship that would otherwise go to her bank.

Data-backed hypothesis

Based on N-06’s account of an unexplained bakery decline that sent her back to her debit card within the week, and an in-store decline rate of 8.7% in Month 4 (against 3.5% overall), I believe that showing a clear reason and retry hint on in-store declines for Alexandra, the active BNPL user who wants pay later in shops, will result in her retrying on Riverty instead of reverting to her debit card, as measured by a 15-point increase in the share of in-store declines followed by a successful Riverty purchase within 7 days. I will protect heavy-user purchases per active month (currently 5.82) and will make a go/no-go decision after 90 days or 100 logged in-store declines, whichever comes later.

Slide 6 · Research

Alexandra’s workaround: back to the debit card

Role

Alexandra, the heavy BNPL user who wants Riverty in the shop

Goal

Use pay later at the supermarket and other shops, without a new thing to check before payday (N-04, N-02).

External tools (workaround)

Fall back to the debit card at the till Pay again with another card after a late notification

The process

  1. Taps or presents the Riverty card at the till (e.g., the bakery).
  2. The payment is declined and the app shows no reason (T-101).
  3. She can’t tell whether the problem is the card, the shop, her limit, or the terminal, so she can’t retry with confidence.
  4. She pays with her debit card instead, in front of the queue.
  5. Within the week, she stops trying the Riverty card in-store (N-06).

Core frustration

1. It costs her the benefit she wanted: the debit card pays now, so she loses the deferral.
2. It adds the “one more thing to check” burden she wants to avoid: two charges across two accounts.

Evidence

“The card was declined at a bakery and the app did not say why. I went back to my debit card the same week.” (N-06) · also N-01, N-02, N-04, T-101, N-03

PRD highlight

A vague brief would say “show the decline reason” and leave out that some reasons can’t be shown. The PRD sends any unmapped, fraud- or credit-related code to a fallback that never states the cause, and limits all copy to a closed list of approved strings keyed by decline code. Open gap: risk and legal must supply the list of decline codes that can’t be disclosed.

Where I overrode the AI

B3 (one balance view): scores switched from 3 and 4 to 4 and 3, since value is higher and effort lower; not cut, done later. B5 (partner offers) and B7 (physical card) were over-valued at 2; no consumer note supports them, so the persona-based score is 1. Both are cut anyway.

Slide 8 · Validation

Experiment plan: reason and retry hint vs. current workaround

Hypothesis

Showing a clear reason and retry hint on in-store declines for Alexandra, the heavy BNPL user who wants Riverty in the shop, will result in her seeing the reason and one next step, fixing it on the spot and retrying with her Riverty Card, instead of reaching for her debit card and drifting away from Riverty.

Control (A)

Current workaround:

  • Fall back to the debit card at the till
  • Pay again with another card after a late notification

Variant (B)

  • Decline notice delivered while she is still at the till
  • Reason and retry hint in the notification text
  • Plain-language reasons for the most common codes, naming the Riverty card
  • One next step per reason
  • Generic fallback; risk and legal approved copy, in German

Likely post-launch decision

ITERATE

Sample is too small to prove +10 points at day 35, so a directional read is the most probable outcome. Double-down needs +10 points with the interval above 0 and all guardrails held; deprioritize if the lift is zero or negative with an adequate sample.

Success-metrics dashboard

1 · Primary metric

Share of in-store declines followed by a successful Riverty purchase within 7 days (variant minus control).

WORKED IF at least +10 points, 95% interval lower bound above 0.

BAD SIGNALS
  • Control rate already high (e.g. above 60%): little headroom. Tighten the definition to in-store card purchases and add the same-day metric.
  • Lift positive but under +10, or interval wide: sample too small. Iterate or use the one pre-agreed extension.
  • Lift zero or negative: kill, then review the notice text.

2 · The moment at the till

Share of in-store declines followed by a successful Riverty payment at the same merchant within 30 minutes.

WORKED IF clearly higher in the variant: the misery at the till was resolved, not just a later purchase.

BAD SIGNALS
  • 7-day metric rises but this one is flat: the gain comes from unrelated purchases. Don’t ship on the 7-day result alone.
  • Flat for mapped codes too: the step or wording doesn’t work. Iterate the copy.

Slide 10 · Story

Friction points, the aha moment, and what’s next

Friction 1 · No baseline

The primary metric has no measured baseline, and 30% is a placeholder. The 8.7% decline rate is easy to mistake for it.

Unblock: back-fill from the 48 logged declines in Week 1; use the control arm as the baseline at the read.

Friction 2 · Volume

At about 12 in-store declines a week (assumption), a 6-week window gives about 48 declines against 356 per arm needed.

Unblock: confirm weekly volume in Week 1, then widen eligibility, accept a larger MDE, or run a directional pilot.

Friction 3 · Test vs. feature

The release is size S, but the test needs about 712 declines. Over-investing costs as much as under-investing.

Unblock: say up front that the first read is directional, and keep the build to the notification.

The aha moment

The read at day 35: you open the results and the decision is obvious. Same-day recovery is up in mapped declines, guardrails are flat, there are zero unsafe notices, and the split was clean. “I don’t need to argue about whether it worked. The numbers and the rules decide.”

Key takeaways

  1. The idea is sound and small. A reason and one next step at a decline fixes a real, evidenced pain (N-06, T-101, 8.7% in-store decline rate in Month 4). It is a size S release, not a launch.
  2. The metric is conversion. A failed in-store payment becomes a successful Riverty purchase. Opens and taps are diagnostics only.
  3. The baseline is the biggest gap. It isn’t in the data pack, and the 8.7% is the decline rate, not the baseline.
  4. The experiment is bigger than the feature. 356 per arm means about 712 declines, so a significant read is unlikely and “iterate” is the most likely decision.

What I would do next

  1. Confirm the weekly in-store decline volume and the period behind the 48 declines.
  2. Back-fill the baseline and fix the metric definition (what counts as a successful Riverty purchase; the “heavy user” threshold).
  3. Choose the sample-size option and name an owner and cost for every activity.
  4. Start the legal review of the German strings in Week 2: the most likely slip.
